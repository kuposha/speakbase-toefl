## 4. Полный конспект

### 4.1. Основная идея и функция конструкции

Advanced adjective and adverb choices make descriptions more precise. Adjectives can classify, evaluate or describe a state; adverbs can modify an adjective, verb, another adverb or an entire proposition. Degree, focus, comment and viewpoint adverbs have different scopes, and their position affects interpretation.

The source sequence in Advanced Grammar in Use Units 66?78 covers adjective position and meaning, gradable/non-gradable adjectives, participle and compound adjectives, adjective complementation, adverb formation and position, comparison, degree/focus, comment and viewpoint adverbs.

### 4.2. Формирование и грамматические модели

**1. Adjective position and meaning**

- Attributive adjectives normally precede the noun: *a reliable method*.
- Predicative adjectives follow linking verbs such as be, seem, become, feel and remain: *The method seems reliable.*
- Some adjectives are mainly attributive or mainly predicative; do not assume every adjective behaves identically.
- A participle adjective may describe an effect/state: **-ing** often describes what causes a feeling (*an interesting result*); **-ed** often describes the experiencer's state (*interested students*). Context and lexical meaning still matter.
- Compound adjectives may be hyphenated before a noun: *a fast-growing sector, a well-known researcher*. Follow established spelling and avoid assuming all compounds take the same pattern.

**2. Gradable and non-gradable adjectives**

- Gradable adjectives allow degrees: *very tired, extremely useful, slightly unusual*.
- Non-gradable adjectives describe an absolute or category-like state in the intended sense; they do not normally combine with **very** in that same sense.
- Intensifiers differ: **absolutely/completely/entirely** often combine with non-gradable adjectives; **very/extremely/fairly/rather** commonly combine with gradable adjectives.
- Some adjectives have both gradable and non-gradable meanings (for example, *certain, common, old*). Check the intended meaning, not just the dictionary label.

**3. Adjective complementation**

An adjective can be followed by:
- **to-infinitive**: *easy to use, difficult to explain*;
- **-ing**: *good at explaining, interested in learning*;
- **that-clause**: *aware that the data are incomplete*;
- **wh-clause**: *uncertain what the result means*;
- **preposition + -ing/noun**: *afraid of making a mistake, responsible for the outcome*.

Some adjectives allow more than one pattern, sometimes with a change of meaning: *afraid to do* (reluctance/fear about taking an action) versus *afraid of doing* (fear of the activity/event). Learn the adjective with its complement pattern.

**4. Adverbs: form and alternatives**

Many adverbs are formed with **-ly**, but not all: *fast, hard, late, well*. Some **-ly** forms have a different meaning from the related adjective (for example, *hard/hardly*). In some contexts, a prepositional phrase can replace an adverb (*in a careful manner*), but the register and meaning may differ.

**5. Comparative and superlative forms**

- Short adjectives/adverbs often take **-er/-est**; longer forms often use **more/most**. There are irregular forms such as *better/best* and *worse/worst*.
- Use **than** to introduce the comparison; use **as + adjective/adverb + as** for equality.
- Comparative phrases can use **much/far/a lot/slightly + comparative**, and structures such as **not as...as**, **the more..., the more...**.
- Avoid double marking (*more easier*). Use the form appropriate to the adjective/adverb and the intended comparison.

**6. Adverb position**

- End position is common for manner, place and time adverbs; an object normally comes before a manner adverb.
- Mid position is common for frequency adverbs and some comment/degree adverbs; position depends on the verb phrase (before a main verb, after be, and after the first auxiliary).
- Front position is common for comment, viewpoint and linking adverbs (*Apparently, ...; From a practical perspective, ...*).
- Place/direction adverbs can be fronted for emphasis; with certain intransitive verbs a full noun subject may follow the verb (*Here comes the bus*). Pronoun subjects normally keep ordinary order (*Here it comes*).

**7. Degree, focus, comment and viewpoint**

- Degree adverbs modify strength: *very, highly, fairly, deeply, slightly, much*.
- Focus adverbs identify what is included/excluded or especially important: *only, even, particularly, mainly, especially*.
- Comment adverbs express the speaker's assessment of a proposition: *fortunately, apparently, arguably, surprisingly*.
- Viewpoint adverbs frame the statement from a perspective: *politically, technically, from an economic perspective*.
- Put each adverb close to the element or proposition it modifies; moving it can change scope.

### 4.3. Как выбрать подходящую форму

1. Decide whether the word describes a noun (adjective) or modifies a verb, adjective, adverb or whole clause (adverb).
2. Check whether the adjective is attributive, predicative, participle-based or part of a compound.
3. Decide whether the intended adjective meaning is gradable; choose an intensifier that matches that meaning.
4. Learn the adjective with its complement: to-infinitive, -ing, that-/wh-clause or preposition + -ing.
5. For comparison, choose comparative/superlative or as...as and check irregular forms and modifiers.
6. Place manner/place/time adverbs naturally at the end; place frequency and other mid-position adverbs according to the verb phrase.
7. Use front position for comment/viewpoint/linking purposes only when the scope is clear.
8. With fronted place/direction adverbs, check the verb and subject type; do not invert automatically after every fronted phrase.

### 4.4. Сравнение с близкими конструкциями

| Contrast | Meaning / rule |
|---|---|
| an interesting lecture / interested listeners | -ing describes what causes the response; -ed describes the experiencer's state |
| very useful / absolutely essential | gradable vs non-gradable meaning and compatible intensifier |
| afraid to speak / afraid of speaking | reluctance/fear about taking the action vs fear of the activity/event |
| hard / hardly | hard = with effort; hardly = almost not |
| She spoke clearly / a clearly written report | adverb modifies a verb vs an adjective/participle |
| He usually works late / He is usually late | mid-position depends on whether the main verb is lexical or be |
| Fortunately, the plan succeeded / The plan was fortunately successful | comment adverb scope can apply to the whole proposition or a narrower element |
| Here comes the bus / Here it comes | place inversion with a full noun subject vs ordinary order with a pronoun subject |

### 4.6. Исключения, ограничения и границы употребления

- Adjective position is partly lexical; some adjectives are mainly attributive or predicative.
- -ing/-ed participle adjectives are a useful tendency, not a universal mechanical rule; verify lexical meaning and context.
- Some adjectives have both gradable and non-gradable senses; intensifier choice follows the intended sense.
- Do not use **very** indiscriminately with non-gradable adjectives; use an appropriate intensifier such as absolutely/completely where suitable.
- Not every adverb ends in -ly; some -ly pairs differ in meaning (hard/hardly, late/lately).
- Adverb position is flexible but not arbitrary. Keep the adverb close to its scope and avoid unintended ambiguity.
- Fronted place/direction adverbs do not always require inversion. The verb type and whether the subject is a full noun phrase or pronoun matter.
- Comparatives require one comparison marker, not double marking; adjective/adverb forms and irregulars must be checked.
- Avoid stacking degree/comment adverbs or using a viewpoint phrase without a clear relation to the proposition.

### 4.7. Типичные ошибки русскоязычных студентов

| `Broad, the findings support the proposed interpretation, although some uncertainty remains.` | `Broadly speaking, the findings support the proposed interpretation, although some uncertainty remains.` | Broad is an adjective and does not introduce this adverbial phrase. |
| `The two estimates are broad comparable, but they are not identical.` | `The two estimates are broadly comparable, but they are not identical.` | Broad is an adjective, not the adverb required before comparable. |
| `Methodological, the sampling procedure introduced a potential source of bias.` | `From a methodological perspective, the sampling procedure introduced a potential source of bias.` | Methodological is an adjective without a complete phrase. |

Русский перевод помогает понять смысл, но не управляет английской формой напрямую. Проверяй роль каждого элемента и грамматическую модель английского предложения.

### 4.12. Краткая памятка

- Adjectives describe nouns/states; adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, adverbs or whole propositions.
- -ing often describes the cause; -ed often describes the experiencer's state.
- Match intensifier to gradable/non-gradable meaning.
- Learn adjective + complement patterns as collocations.
- Comparatives: -er/more + than; equality: as...as; avoid double marking.
- Adverb position: end (often manner/place/time), mid (frequency and other operators), front (comment/viewpoint/linking).
- Place inversion is restricted; full noun subjects may invert after certain place expressions, pronouns normally do not.
- Check scope: the position of only/even/fortunately/apparently can change what is being modified.
